## Choose questions worth answering

If I ask to plan first, stop after a timestamped plan of ten promising scene details and why each matters. Flag any conflict between the narration and the source. Draft the full set when I ask to create it.

Interestingness is the top selection priority. Accuracy and relevance are requirements. Every question must have one verifiable answer and connect directly to what the viewer is hearing at that timestamp. Among the questions that qualify, choose the one a fan would most enjoy answering.

Read the whole video first, including the opening hook and conclusion. For each promising moment, consider several angles. Look for a surprising second detail, two characters who want different things from the same encounter, an earlier line whose meaning changes, or a canon answer stranger than the obvious guess.

Use two tests:

1. Before the reveal, would someone who understands the setup actually want to guess?
2. After the reveal, would a fan who already knew the answer enjoy what the question made them notice?

A useful pattern is to give the viewer the headline action and ask for the detail that changes its meaning. For example, once the narration says a father told his son to steal, ask how he answered when the child worried about getting caught. The answer reveals how he pressured his son. Look for other angles across the set: an offer someone misunderstood, the next line after a famous quote, or an earlier conversation that changes what a character knows now. Choose a detail whose significance you can explain without reaching.

## Choose the timestamp

- Format timestamps as minutes:seconds.frames (m:ss.ff), e.g. 0:46.00 or 4:05.00. Use total minutes and two digits each for seconds and frames. Use .00 for whole-second placements. Convert subsecond positions only with the relevant timeline frame rate. Use the video clock for placement and the episode clock for sources.
- For a video around 20–25 minutes, aim for roughly 2–3 minutes between quizzes. For other lengths, adapt the spacing to the video’s natural beats.
- Place each quiz after viewers have enough context to understand the setup. If the narration supplies the answer, place the quiz at a natural break just before the reveal.
- If the narration gives away the obvious question, move one beat later. Use the revealed fact as shared context and ask about a meaningful detail the narration leaves open.
- Check for earlier giveaways, especially in the opening hook. When revisiting something viewers have already heard, reward them for making a new connection.
- Give emotional lines, punchlines, and the ending room to land. The last quiz can set up the conclusion several minutes before the video ends.

## Write the questions and choices

Write all viewer-facing questions, choices, and explanations from the story itself. Never refer to the video, essay, narration, narrator, or what the viewer has just watched or heard. Ground a comparison or interpretation in named characters, actions, dialogue, or scenes. Reserve editorial context for creator notes.

For an argument or callback, name the accusation, identify the incident being invoked, and ask about the specific action or consequence at issue. Read the stem without the timestamp, options, or explanation. If it could fit several exchanges, or just asks whose name came up without giving the player a reason to care, rewrite it.

- Read each question as if it appeared alone on a phone. Name the people and the specific exchange, episode, or situation needed to understand it. Remove vague references and words or quoted fragments that give away the answer.
- Give exactly four short choices with no punctuation at the end of any option. Put the answer key on a separate line so all four options can be pasted as written. Vary the correct position across the set.
- Write three credible, distinct distractors that answer the same question in the same grammatical form. Check that none is also defensible. Avoid three variations on one idea alongside a correct option in a completely different category.
- Hide the answer key and try to solve the question as a viewer who forgot the scene. If length, specificity, tone, a matching word, character stereotypes, or the only punchline gives it away, rewrite the choices. If the underlying fact is still obvious, replace the question.
- Plan the viewer’s task as well as the topic. Mix prediction, comparing two perspectives, interpreting a line from observable evidence, spotting a parallel, and selecting the detail that supports the essay. Keep a few rewarding recall questions. Changing which episode a question cites does not change the task. Keep titles in creator notes unless the viewer needs one to identify the scene. For interpretive questions, ground the key in a line, action, or the narrator’s stated argument; keep genuinely debatable readings for discussion.
- Escalate the humor across the set. Start straight, introduce dry humor, and let the final questions get genuinely unhinged. Build the comedy into how the question describes the actual situation. If removing the joke leaves a dull question, find a better angle.
- Respect the tone of the specific scene. If a serious moment falls late in the video, place the joke on a nearby beat where it works.

## Verify and explain

Start with the reveal: write one sentence saying what a viewer will enjoy discovering. Identify a fair way into the question for someone who recognizes the scene but cannot quote it. Balance accessible and harder questions independently of the escalating humor.

Verify the entire premise against the primary scene, including the speaker, setting, sequence and action. Then review each question once at normal reading speed with the key hidden. Choose an answer, make the strongest case for the runner-up, and check what in the scene rules it out. If the wording tips the answer, two choices survive, or the reveal requires a technical defense, rewrite or replace the question.

### Fair challenge: no trick questions

- Make the challenge come from remembering or noticing something meaningful in the scene. Use ordinary meanings a viewer can apply on first reading. Avoid hidden qualifiers, double negatives, and distinctions that depend on a technical definition.
- Compare the four answers in pairs. Reject umbrella terms alongside their possible members (such as “estate” alongside individual holdings), synonyms, partial restatements, and combinations where one option includes another. Exclude “all of the above” and “none of the above.”
- Privately read each option as an answer to the exact question. For each wrong choice, identify what in the scene rules it out. If an attentive fan could reasonably defend a second choice in everyday language, replace it. If the question needs a longer disclaimer to make it fair, choose another detail at that timestamp.
- Use “first,” “exactly,” or “technically” only when the sequence or wording changes how we understand the scene. If the only payoff is catching someone who remembers several true things, drop the question. The explanation should reward the viewer with an insight into the scene.

- Check each answer against the supplied material. For quotation questions, verify the exact words. If the video paraphrases a line, use the source wording for a direct quote.
- When motives are ambiguous, ask about an observable action or line. The explanation can explore the narrator’s interpretation while preserving its uncertainty.
- Write a viewer-facing explanation of 400–500 characters for each quiz, aiming close to 500 where the detail earns it. Never exceed 500. Count the final text, including spaces and punctuation, excluding the label and displayed count.
- Answer the question, then show why that detail matters at this point in the video. Use concrete details and the narrator’s conversational voice. Preserve discoveries the video is saving for later.
